Santiago de Cuba at a glance

Among the 125 towns ClimaClue covers in Cuba, Santiago de Cuba is the 2nd least sunny (about 2,367 hours of sunshine a year). Compared with San Luis, the nearest covered town with different numbers (19 km away), Santiago de Cuba is 0.9 °C warmer in July and 1.2 °C warmer in January, with 260 mm less precipitation a year. Its warmest month, July (28.2 °C), averages 3.7 °C more than its coolest, January (24.4 °C). Even the coolest nights average 19.1 °C (66 °F), in January. The area lies about 22 km from the sea (coastal).

Station check: no NOAA GHCN-Daily station with 1981–2010 averages lies within 25 km of Santiago de Cuba, so these modelled values have not been compared with a nearby station.

Best time to visit Santiago de Cuba

For mild weather, the best months to visit Santiago de Cuba are January, February, March and December.

  • Mild (average temperature 15–25 °C / 59–77 °F and under 100 mm / 3.9 in of precipitation): January, February, March and December.
  • Warmest (average temperature): July (28.2 °C), August (28.1 °C) and June (27.5 °C).
  • Driest (least precipitation): January (27 mm), February (33 mm) and December (33 mm).
  • Sunniest (hours of sunshine, about 1961–1990): April (225 h), July (224 h) and March (220 h).

Why Santiago de Cuba has this climate

Typical mechanisms for this climate type, plus facts measured for this place; not a verified local cause.

It stays warm all year; the dry season brings clearer skies and wider day–night swings. The tropical rain belt follows the sun: it brings a wet season when overhead and a long dry season when it moves away. On the coast: the sea warms and cools slowly, which usually softens seasonal swings. Here the coolest and warmest months differ by 3.7 °C. Within the 10 km area around it, monthly temperatures differ by up to 3.1 °C — typical where height changes quickly, as in hills or mountains.

Strongly seasonal: the wettest month gets more than five times the driest.

About these numbers

Temperature, precipitation and wind: modelled monthly averages (climate normals) for Santiago de Cuba itself, the average of the ~1 km source cells in the 3 × 3 km around its GeoNames location (20.023°N, 75.822°W). Day highs and night lows add the day–night range of the surrounding CRU cell (about 18 km), or on a few small islands the nearest weather station’s; where there is neither, they are not shown. Sunshine, dew point and snow come from coarser sources and describe the 0.1° area around it, about 10 × 11 km (ClimaClue area sq-639-1041), which is also what the globe shows when you click there; humidity is calculated from that dew point and the town’s own night lows and day highs. They are model estimates, not measurements at a weather station, and not forecasts. Sources: CHELSA V2.1, 1981–2010 (temperature, precipitation, wind); ERA5-Land, 1981–2010 (dew point and snow; snowfall matched to the area’s CHELSA climate; contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information); relative humidity calculated from the dew point and the night lows and day highs; CRU CL 2.0, about 1961–1990 (sunshine and the day–night range; a different period); GeoNames (name and population 555,865, CC BY 4.0); climate type by the Köppen–Geiger rules of Peel et al.
