Alice Springs at a glance

Among the 89 towns ClimaClue covers in Australia, Alice Springs is the 4th sunniest (about 3,385 hours of sunshine a year) and the 3rd driest (276 mm a year). Compared with Mount Isa, the nearest covered town with different numbers (666 km away), Alice Springs is 1.4 °C cooler in January and 5.4 °C cooler in July, with 179 mm less precipitation a year. Its warmest month, January (29.4 °C), averages 17.4 °C more than its coolest, July (11.9 °C). No month has an average night low below 0 °C (32 °F); the coldest, July, averages 3.9 °C (single nights can be colder). The area lies about 904 km from the sea (deep inland).

Station check: the nearest NOAA GHCN-Daily station with 1981–2010 averages, 11 km away, agrees with these values: monthly temperatures within 0.9 °C of the station’s, yearly precipitation 95% of the station’s, and a similar seasonal rain pattern. Temperatures on this page are then adjusted toward nearby NOAA stations at a similar height, so they follow the measurements more closely.

Best time to visit Alice Springs

For mild weather, the best months to visit Alice Springs are April, May, September and October.

  • Mild (average temperature 15–25 °C / 59–77 °F and under 100 mm / 3.9 in of precipitation): April, May, September and October.
  • Warmest (average temperature): January (29.4 °C), February (28.3 °C) and December (28.1 °C).

Why Alice Springs has this climate

Typical mechanisms for this climate type, plus facts measured for this place; not a verified local cause.

Clear, dry air lets the ground heat strongly by day and lose heat quickly at night, so swings are large. Sinking air in the subtropical high-pressure belt, distance from moist air, or mountains blocking it keeps rain rare. About 904 km from the sea: deep inland, where land heats and cools quickly, which usually makes seasons extreme. Here the coolest and warmest months differ by 17.4 °C.

Strongly seasonal: the wettest month gets more than five times the driest.

About these numbers

Temperature, precipitation and wind: modelled monthly averages (climate normals) for Alice Springs itself, the average of the ~1 km source cells in the 3 × 3 km around its GeoNames location (23.697°S, 133.884°E). Day highs and night lows add the day–night range of the surrounding CRU cell (about 18 km), or on a few small islands the nearest weather station’s; where there is neither, they are not shown. Sunshine, dew point and snow come from coarser sources and describe the 0.1° area around it, about 10 × 11 km (ClimaClue area sq-1076-3138), which is also what the globe shows when you click there; humidity is calculated from that dew point and the town’s own night lows and day highs. They are model estimates, not measurements at a weather station, and not forecasts. Sources: CHELSA V2.1, 1981–2010 (temperature, precipitation, wind); ERA5-Land, 1981–2010 (dew point and snow; snowfall matched to the area’s CHELSA climate; contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information); relative humidity calculated from the dew point and the night lows and day highs; CRU CL 2.0, about 1961–1990 (sunshine and the day–night range; a different period); GeoNames (name and population 25,912, CC BY 4.0); climate type by the Köppen–Geiger rules of Peel et al. (2007). How ClimaClue makes these numbers.
