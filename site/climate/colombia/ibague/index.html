Ibagué at a glance

Among the 55 towns of 100,000+ people covered in Colombia, Ibagué is 25th warmest (22.4 °C year average), 36th sunniest (about 1,698 hours of sunshine a year) and 17th wettest (2,049 mm a year), near the middle on each. Compared with Girardot City, the nearest covered town with different numbers (46 km away), Ibagué is 2.9 °C cooler in September and 4.1 °C cooler in December, with 255 mm more precipitation a year. Its warmest month, September (22.9 °C), averages 1.0 °C more than its coolest, December (21.9 °C). Even the coolest nights average 17.8 °C (64 °F), in January. The area lies about 235 km from the sea.

Station check: no NOAA GHCN-Daily station with 1981–2010 averages lies within 25 km of Ibagué, so these modelled values have not been compared with a nearby station.

Why Ibagué has this climate

Typical mechanisms for this climate type, plus facts measured for this place; not a verified local cause.

Near the equator the midday sun is high all year, so temperatures barely change between months. Warm, moist air rises almost every day near the equatorial rain belt, building showers and thunderstorms, so no month is truly dry. About 235 km from the sea: inland, so ocean moderation is weaker and seasons are usually sharper. Here the coolest and warmest months differ by 1.0 °C. The 10 km area around Ibagué, which the globe shows when you click there, averages 19.9 °C a year, 2.5 °C cooler than the town itself, because it takes in higher ground.

Rain or snow is spread through much of the year.

About these numbers

Temperature, day highs and night lows, precipitation and wind: modelled monthly averages (climate normals) for Ibagué itself, the average of the ~1 km source cells in the 3 × 3 km around its GeoNames location (4.436°N, 75.203°W). Sunshine, dew point and snow come from coarser sources and describe the 0.1° area around it, about 11 × 11 km (ClimaClue area sq-795-1047), which is also what the globe shows when you click there; humidity is calculated from that dew point and the town’s own night lows and day highs. They are model estimates, not measurements at a weather station, and not forecasts.
