Nazca at a glance

Among the 136 towns ClimaClue covers in Peru, Nazca is 38th warmest (22.4 °C year average), 19th sunniest (about 2,679 hours of sunshine a year) and 96th wettest (73 mm a year), near the middle on each. Compared with Minas de Marcona, the nearest covered town with different numbers (46 km away), Nazca is 3.9 °C warmer in March and 3.3 °C warmer in July, with 46 mm more precipitation a year. Its warmest month, March (24.5 °C), averages 4.8 °C more than its coolest, July (19.7 °C). Even the coolest nights average 14.8 °C (59 °F), in July. The area lies about 62 km from the sea.

Station check: no NOAA GHCN-Daily station with 1981–2010 averages lies within 25 km of Nazca, so these modelled values have not been compared with a nearby station.

Why Nazca has this climate

Typical mechanisms for this climate type, plus facts measured for this place; not a verified local cause.

Clear, dry air lets the ground heat strongly by day and lose heat quickly at night, so swings are large. Sinking air in the subtropical high-pressure belt, distance from moist air, or mountains blocking it keeps rain rare. About 62 km from the sea: close enough for ocean air to moderate the seasons on many days. Here the coolest and warmest months differ by 4.8 °C. Within the 10 km area around it, monthly temperatures differ by up to 3.5 °C — typical where height changes quickly, as in hills or mountains.

Strongly seasonal: the wettest month gets more than five times the driest.

About these numbers

Temperature, precipitation and wind: modelled monthly averages (climate normals) for Nazca itself, the average of the ~1 km source cells in the 3 × 3 km around its GeoNames location (14.831°S, 74.939°W). Day highs and night lows add the day–night range of the surrounding CRU cell (about 18 km), or on a few small islands the nearest weather station’s; where there is neither, they are not shown. Sunshine, dew point and snow come from coarser sources and describe the 0.1° area around it, about 11 × 11 km (ClimaClue area sq-988-1050), which is also what the globe shows when you click there; humidity is calculated from that dew point and the town’s own night lows and day highs. They are model estimates, not measurements at a weather station, and not forecasts. Sources: CHELSA V2.1, 1981–2010 (temperature, precipitation, wind); ERA5-Land, 1981–2010 (dew point and snow; snowfall matched to the area’s CHELSA climate; contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information); relative humidity calculated from the dew point and the night lows and day highs; CRU CL 2.0, about 1961–1990 (sunshine and the day–night range; a different period); GeoNames (name and population 23,556, CC BY 4.0); climate type by the Köppen–Geiger rules of Peel et al. (2007). How ClimaClue makes these numbers.
