Rioja at a glance

Among the 136 towns ClimaClue covers in Peru, Rioja is 34th warmest (23.1 °C year average), 78th sunniest (about 2,070 hours of sunshine a year) and 22nd wettest (1,417 mm a year), near the middle on each. Compared with Moyobamba, the nearest covered town with different numbers (21 km away), Rioja is 0.9 °C warmer in November and 0.9 °C warmer in July, with 18 mm more precipitation a year. Its warmest month, November (23.7 °C), averages 1.7 °C more than its coolest, July (22.1 °C). Even the coolest nights average 15.9 °C (61 °F), in July. The area lies about 312 km from the sea.

Station check: no NOAA GHCN-Daily station with 1981–2010 averages lies within 25 km of Rioja, so these modelled values have not been compared with a nearby station.

Best time to visit Rioja

For mild weather, the best months to visit Rioja are June, July, August and September.

  • Mild (average temperature 15–25 °C / 59–77 °F and under 100 mm / 3.9 in of precipitation): June, July, August and September.
  • Warmest (average temperature): November (23.7 °C), December (23.7 °C) and January (23.6 °C).
  • Driest (least precipitation): July (55 mm), August (66 mm) and June (70 mm).
  • Sunniest (hours of sunshine, about 1961–1990): August (202 h), July (196 h) and September (182 h).

Why Rioja has this climate

Typical mechanisms for this climate type, plus facts measured for this place; not a verified local cause.

The tropical sun keeps it warm all year; the hottest weeks often come just before the rains. Seasonal monsoon winds bring a very wet season, and a short dry season follows when they reverse. About 312 km from the sea: inland, so ocean moderation is weaker and seasons are usually sharper. Here the coolest and warmest months differ by 1.7 °C.

Rain or snow is spread through much of the year.

About these numbers

Temperature, precipitation and wind: modelled monthly averages (climate normals) for Rioja itself, the average of the ~1 km source cells in the 3 × 3 km around its GeoNames location (6.057°S, 77.167°W). Day highs and night lows add the day–night range of the surrounding CRU cell (about 18 km), or on a few small islands the nearest weather station’s; where there is neither, they are not shown. Sunshine, dew point and snow come from coarser sources and describe the 0.1° area around it, about 11 × 11 km (ClimaClue area sq-900-1028), which is also what the globe shows when you click there; humidity is calculated from that dew point and the town’s own night lows and day highs. They are model estimates, not measurements at a weather station, and not forecasts. Sources: CHELSA V2.1, 1981–2010 (temperature, precipitation, wind); ERA5-Land, 1981–2010 (dew point and snow; snowfall matched to the area’s CHELSA climate; contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information); relative humidity calculated from the dew point and the night lows and day highs; CRU CL 2.0, about 1961–1990 (sunshine and the day–night range; a different period); GeoNames (name and population 25,057, CC BY 4.0); climate type by the Köppen–Geiger rules of Peel et al. (2007). How ClimaClue makes these numbers.
