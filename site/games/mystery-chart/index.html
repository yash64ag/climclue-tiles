Mystery Chart: guess the city from its climate

Free daily game

A daily geography and climate game. You see one real town’s chart of monthly temperature and precipitation, with no name, plus a few clues. Pick which of four world regions it is in.

Play now ›

How to play

The chart shows twelve months of average temperature as a line and precipitation (rain and snow combined) as bars. Read it like a climate scientist: a hot, flat temperature line means the tropics; a big swing between summer and winter means a continental interior; a summer peak in July means the northern hemisphere, a peak in January the southern. Dry summers and wet winters suggest a Mediterranean climate; almost no rain at all, a desert.

Below the chart are clues from the town’s other measures: its yearly sunshine, its snowfall, and its sea temperature or distance from the sea. Choose one of four world regions, lettered A to D on the globe. You have two tries. A wrong pick tells you why: it names that region’s closest climate to the mystery chart (with a match percentage) and how the mystery place differs, such as “warmer and much wetter”, and it unlocks one more clue.

Right first time scores 5,000 points, on the second try 2,500. At the end the town is shown on the globe with its climate type, a short explanation of why it has that climate, and why each of the other regions was wrong. There is one daily set, the same for everyone on the same date, and it changes at your local midnight. Your result can be shared as a short spoiler-free line.

What you learn

Mystery Chart trains the skill of reading a climate graph (a climograph), which is part of most geography and earth science courses. After a few games the shapes become familiar: the double rain peak near the equator, the summer monsoon, the cold-winter steppe. It works well in a classroom: the daily set is the same for every student on the same date, a round takes a few minutes, and every answer comes with a short explanation to discuss.

Where the charts come from

Place-based games use towns of 100,000+ people. Each town’s values were compared with the nearest NOAA weather station that has 1981–2010 averages, and towns whose values disagree with that station are left out, so a round never rests on a doubtful number. Temperature and precipitation are CHELSA V2.1 monthly averages for 1981–2010, worked out for areas of about 10 km (0.1°) on the globe, and for the few kilometres around each town’s centre on town pages, in the games and in the Matchmaker. Day highs and night lows take that average with the day–night range of CRU CL 2.0, sunshine comes from CRU CL 2.0 too (about 1961–1990), and snow from ERA5-Land (1981–2010). They are modelled climate averages (normals), not weather forecasts or station records.

Questions and answers

How do you play Mystery Chart?
Look at a real town’s monthly temperature and precipitation chart and its clues, then pick which of four world regions it is in. You get two tries, and a wrong pick explains why and adds a clue.
Is there a new Mystery Chart every day?
Yes. Everyone gets the same town on the same date, and a new one starts at local midnight.
Are the mystery towns real?
Yes. Every chart is a real town of 100,000+ people, using CHELSA 1981–2010 monthly averages for the town itself, and towns that disagree with a nearby weather station are left out.
Is Mystery Chart free?
Yes, the daily game is free to play in a web browser.
